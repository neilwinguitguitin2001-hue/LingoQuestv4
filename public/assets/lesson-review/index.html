Past Perfect Tense

Grade 7 · Quarter 1 · Module 5 · g7-q1-m5

Editorial source · CO_Q1_ENGLISH 7_MODULE 5.pdf · PDF pages 9, 10 (counted from the cover).

Concepts & Rules

Original teaching example

See it in action

At 7:10 the bus left. At 7:15 Mia arrived.

Learn the rule

Use had plus the past participle to mark an action already finished before another point in the past. A past participle is the verb form used after had, such as left or finished.

Walk through an example

The bus had left before Mia arrived. Had left marks the 7:10 event. Arrived gives the later past event.

Notice a common mistake

Had went is incorrect. Go changes to gone after had.

Remember the key idea

Past perfect looks back from a point in the past.

Original teaching example: The shop had closed before I reached the door. First, the shop closed. Later, I reached the door. Compare “The shop closed, and I reached the door.” The simple past reports two events in sequence. The past perfect explicitly looks back from the later arrival.

Concepts & Rules

Original teaching example

See it in action

A bar chart shows 12 library loans on Monday, 18 on Tuesday and 15 on Wednesday.

Learn the rule

A visual–verbal relationship connects what a visual shows with what words explain. Check the title, labels, unit and values before writing a caption.

Walk through an example

“Tuesday had the most loans of the three days” is supported by 18, compared with 12 and 15. “Tuesday is always busiest” goes beyond the dates shown.

Notice a common mistake

Do not confuse loans with borrowers or three days with a usual pattern.

Remember the key idea

Say exactly what the visual supports.

Original teaching example: A room table lists 24 chairs in A and 18 in B. “Room A has six more chairs than Room B” follows from 24 minus 18. “Room A has more seats than any room in the school” would need data on other rooms.

Using Conditionals to Express Arguments (Third Conditional Sentences)

Grade 9 · Quarter 1 · Week 5 · g9-q1-w5

Editorial source · EDITED-G9-SLEM-Q1-W5.pdf · PDF pages 5, 6 (counted from the cover).

The objective on PDF p. 2 says future result. The instructional discussion on pp. 5–6 teaches an unreal past condition and imagined past result.

Concepts & Rules

Original teaching example

See it in action

Mia submitted the file after the deadline. Imagine she had submitted it earlier.

Learn the rule

A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. In the third conditional, the if-clause imagines a different past using had plus past participle. The result clause uses would, could or might have plus past participle.

Walk through an example

“If Mia had submitted the file before noon, it would have arrived on time.” Had submitted imagines a different action. Would have arrived shows the imagined result.

Notice a common mistake

“If Mia would have submitted” puts the result form in the if-clause. Use if Mia had submitted.

Remember the key idea

The past stays the same. The sentence imagines what could have changed.

Original teaching example: The team missed its bus after leaving late. “If the team had left earlier, it might have caught the bus.” Might have expresses a possible imagined result. Reversing the clauses also works: “The team might have caught the bus if it had left earlier.” The comma usually drops out when the if-clause comes second.

Visual direction

Alt text / text equivalent. Actual timeline beside a dashed imagined timeline with both clauses labeled. Mia submitted the file after the deadline. Imagine she had submitted it earlier.

Concepts & Rules

Original teaching example

See it in action

In a written scene, Noor thinks the rehearsal room is empty. In the clip, she stops when she hears a chair scrape.

Learn the rule

An adaptation tells a version of a work in another medium. Compare a matched moment and explain how each medium communicates information through words, sound, framing or performance.

Walk through an example

The passage lets us hear Noor’s mistaken assumption as a thought. The clip uses sound and her reaction to make the audience discover someone may be there at the same time she does.

Notice a common mistake

Listing changes without explaining their effect leaves the comparison unfinished.

Remember the key idea

Name the change, cite evidence from both versions and explain its effect.

Original written passage for teacher review: “Noor pushed the rehearsal-room door open. The lights were off, so she was sure everyone had gone home. Then a chair scraped behind the curtain. ‘Who’s there?’ she whispered.” In the clip, the audience sees the dark room and hears the scrape without first hearing Noor’s thought. The written version gives direct access to her assumption. The clip uses the sound and her reaction to let viewers infer it.
